Background

The Learning and Academic Engagement (LAE) team in Te Mātāpuna Library and Learning Services supports students' academic and information literacy development. As part of this work, the team partners with academic staff to embed learning support within courses through workshops and online resources such as Cogniti agents, course-specific AI chatbots designed to support particular learning and assessment tasks.

HEAL506 Knowledge, Enquiry and Communication is a first-year health paper in which students complete an Evidence-based Enquiry Report. The assessment requires students to engage with health information, locate and evaluate scholarly evidence, and apply that evidence to an Aotearoa New Zealand health context. Source evaluation is a key component of the report, requiring students to assess the credibility and relevance of a scholarly source using the Te Whatu Aho Rau framework.

In late 2025, the HEAL506 teaching team and the LAE team collaborated to develop the Te Whatu Aho Rau Source Evaluator. The agent was embedded within the HEAL506 Canvas site during Semesters 1 and 2 of 2026 as an optional online resource to support this component of the assessment. The project also forms part of a broader initiative aimed to explore the efficient use of Cogniti agents to scaffold source evaluation skills across health programmes.

Te Whatu Aho Rau Source Evaluator

The Source Evaluator is a Cogniti agent that supports students in evaluating a scholarly source for Section 2 of the Evidence-based Enquiry Report. It guides students through the four elements of the Te Whatu Aho Rau framework:

  • Pūtaketanga (origin)
  • Aronga (purpose and perspective)
  • Tātai Hononga (connections)
  • Māramatanga (value)

The agent draws on the HEAL506 written assessment instructions and rubric, the Te Whatu Aho Rau Evaluation Framework, and a purpose-built Cogniti system message developed for the task to provide assessment-aligned guidance.

Students can use the agent at different stages of the evaluation process: to work through the framework step by step, strengthen a partially completed evaluation, or receive feedback on a completed evaluation table that students are required to submit as part of the assessment.

The agent is designed around a guided, question-based approach. Rather than evaluating a source for students, it prompts them to identify evidence from their chosen article or source, reflect on its relevance to an Aotearoa New Zealand health context, and make their own credibility judgements. Students are asked to support their reasoning with evidence from the source itself before moving through the evaluation process. Responses are written in plain language and structured to support concise evaluation writing appropriate for the assessment.

Data collection and analysis

This evaluation draws on two sources of data:

  • Chatbot usage records: All conversations held with the agent in HEAL506 across both semesters: 2,630 conversations and 25,076 messages from 883 students.
  • Student Survey: An anonymous Qualtrics survey of Semester 1 students, conducted in June 2026 (n = 89). Students were asked eight questions:
  1. Did the chatbot help you learn how to evaluate scholarly sources for your assessment?
  2. What did the chatbot do well, and what would you improve or change?
  3. Did the four Te Whatu Aho Rau elements encourage you to think more deeply about your source?
  4. Was the support from the chatbot enough on its own, or would you have liked other kinds of support as well?
  5. What other kinds of support would have helped?
  6. Would a chatbot like this be useful in your other health science courses?
  7. What kinds of courses or tasks would a tool like this be most useful for?
  8. Is there anything else you'd like us to know about using the chatbot?

The four open-text survey questions were analysed using inductive coding. Responses were read individually, assigned descriptive codes, and grouped into broader categories. Because a single response could receive multiple codes, category totals may exceed the number of respondents.

Usage records were analysed at the conversation level. Timestamps were converted from UTC to New Zealand time, and conversations were categorised according to their starting mode.

No survey was conducted in Semester 2. Consequently, findings about students' perceptions of the agent are drawn from the Semester 1 survey only, while comparisons between semesters rely solely on chatbot usage-record data.

Survey percentages are reported as a proportion of respondents to a given question. Usage percentages are reported as a proportion of conversations within the relevant semester.

Findings

The findings draw on two sources: usage records from the agent and survey responses from Semester 1 students. They are presented in four parts: Semester 1 usage patterns, Semester 1 survey responses, Semester 2 usage patterns, and a comparison across the two semesters.

Semester 1 usage patterns

USAGE RECORDS · 2 MARCH TO 24 APRIL 2026

The Semester 1 dataset comprised 2,291 conversations and 19,518 messages from 751 of the 1,133 enrolled students. The assessment was due on Thursday 2 April 2026.

A total of 751 students (66% of the cohort) used the agent at least once, indicating substantial uptake for an optional support tool. Most students engaged briefly: 36% had a single conversation, 50% had two conversations or fewer, and 77% used the agent during a single week only.

Use across the semester

Use was concentrated immediately before the due date. A total of 1,280 conversations (56% of the semester total) took place in the week beginning 30 March, and 517 students used the agent in that week alone. Half of all conversations took place in the final three days before submission, and 56% of students used the agent for the first time in those three days.

Use increased progressively as the assessment deadline approached, with activity peaking in the week of submission. Following the due date, use declined substantially, suggesting that students engaged with the agent primarily as an assessment support tool rather than as a general learning resource.

When students used it

A total of 1,170 conversations (51%) took place outside standard working hours. More students used the agent on a Saturday than on a Friday, and 198 conversations began between midnight and 6am.

The student-level picture is clearer still. A total of 258 students (34% of users) never accessed the agent during weekday working hours. For these students, the agent provided support that would not otherwise have been available through conventional staffed library services.

How long conversations ran

Most conversations were short. The most common length was four to six messages, which is two or three exchanges, and 556 conversations (24%) ended after a single question and answer.

Conversation length varied according to how students used the agent: students seeking feedback on a completed table typically needed only a few exchanges, whereas students working through the framework step by step remained engaged for substantially longer.

How students used the agent

Feedback on a completed evaluation table was by far the most common use, accounting for 61% of all conversations. Step-by-step guidance represented 20% of conversations, while requests to improve a partially completed evaluation accounted for a further 6%.

Although feedback on completed work was the most common use, many students also engaged with the agent as a learning tool. A total of 259 students (34% of users) accessed the step-by-step guidance mode at least once, indicating that students used the agent for both formative learning and feedback purposes.

These modes represent different forms of support. Feedback on a completed table functions primarily as a review service, whereas step-by-step guidance is intended to guide students step by step on how to apply the framework. Fifty-one percent of step-by-step conversations progressed through all four framework elements before ending, while others concluded earlier in the process. The usage records do not indicate why students chose to stop at particular points.

Use outside the agent's intended purpose

Not all conversations aligned with the agent's intended purpose. A total of 284 conversations (12% of the semester total) focused on tasks other than source evaluation.

A total of 151 conversations (53% of the 284 conversations outside the agent’s intended scope) related to Section 3 of the assessment. Students were asked to use Copilot Chat to generate an AI summary of their article, and many instead pasted the prescribed prompt into the Te Whato Aho Rau Source Evaluator agent. In total, 195 conversations from 119 students included this prompt at some point in the interaction, and 17 students mentioned Copilot Chat by name.

This highlights the importance of clearly communicating the different purposes of AI tools used within the course. The agent was designed to support source evaluation, but students did not always distinguish it from other AI tools used in the course. Of the 195 replies to that prompt, only four directed the student elsewhere: nothing in the agent's design instructs it to decline work outside its scope.

Only five of the 2,291 conversations contained any attempt to have the agent complete assessment writing on the student's behalf. Within this dataset, students overwhelmingly used the agent as a source of guidance rather than a means of circumventing assessment requirements.

“I want advice not you to write it for me.”

Student, Semester 1 conversation log

Semester 1 survey responses

SURVEY · JUNE 2026 · 89 RESPONDENTS

Eighty-nine students responded, from a cohort of 1,133. The usage records show what students did; the survey asked the same cohort what they made of it.

Did the agent help them learn?

A total of 65 respondents (77% of those who answered the question) reported that the agent helped them learn source evaluation, with 77% selecting either "yes, a little" or "yes, a lot". The most common response was "yes, a little" (52%), while a further 25% reported that it helped "a lot". A total of 17 respondents (20%) said the agent did not help or did not help much.

What did it do well?

Students most often described the agent as something that responded to work they had already done, or that made an unfamiliar task easier to understand. Thirteen described it giving them feedback, ten described it making things clearer, and eight described it explaining what source evaluation involves. Seventy-three of the 89 respondents answered this question.

Some students valued the agent for providing direct guidance, suggestions, or information they could use in their work. Several responses also suggested that students interpreted the agent's purpose more broadly than intended, including expectations that it could help find sources. Finding sources is not a function the agent provides. By contrast, five students described it prompting them to think more deeply, working with the Te Whatu Aho Rau elements, or teaching them something they could reuse independently.

This pattern broadly aligns with the usage records. Students most often described the agent as providing practical support, feedback, clarification, or task guidance, while fewer explicitly referred to deeper learning or transferable evaluation skills.

“The chatbot made the process clearer and easier to follow by breaking down key criteria like credibility, relevance, and reliability.”

Student 70

“It helped me understand what I needed to work on through questions that made me think deeper about my sources.”

Student 64

What would they change?

Accuracy was the most commonly reported concern. Eleven students said the agent gave wrong, misleading or invented information. While this represents a minority of respondents, it highlights the importance of helping students critically evaluate AI-generated responses rather than accepting them at face value. Sixty-seven of the 89 respondents answered this question.

Four students could not find the agent on Canvas, three found it repetitive, three found its feedback too general, and three found the layout hard to work with. Twelve students said there was nothing they would change. Some responses were brief, making it difficult to determine whether they reflected satisfaction, limited engagement with the question, or other reasons for not suggesting improvements.

“Gave me some suggestions that didn't make sense or were the opposite to the teacher.”

Student 74

“I still needed to review the sources myself to ensure accuracy.”

Student 24

The role of prior teaching

Asked what they would change, Student 72 answered: “Nothing, it was of assistance, but only because our lecturers and tutors had given us well rounded explanations of the evaluation framework, providing questions (particularly our tutor) to provoke our thinking as we learned.”

Although this is a single response, it highlights a theme that appears elsewhere in the survey: students often saw the agent as most useful when combined with teaching, discussion, and tutor support rather than as a replacement for them.

Did the four elements prompt deeper thinking?

This is the strongest positive result in the survey. A total of 70 students (82% of those who answered the question) said the Te Whatu Aho Rau elements prompted deeper thinking about their source than they usually would, and 42% chose the strongest option: it made them consider things they would not have thought of before.

Reconciling the prompted and unprompted responses

When asked directly, 82% said the four elements prompted deeper thinking. When asked openly what the agent did well, only five students mentioned deeper thinking, the framework, or learning something they could reuse.

The likely explanation is that the two questions measure different things. Question 3 names the framework and asks students to agree or disagree; Question 2 asks what comes to mind first. Both results are real: the framework did prompt deeper thinking when students were pointed at it, and it was not what most students reached for when describing the experience unprompted. One interpretation is that students noticed the benefits of the framework when asked about it directly, but did not always explicitly attribute those benefits to the framework in open-ended responses. Students may need it named in class to notice they are using it.

Was the agent enough on its own?

Most students viewed the agent as one component of a broader support ecosystem rather than as a standalone source of support. Sixty-nine said they wanted other kinds of support as well, or needed other support more.

What other support would have helped?

Worked examples were the most commonly requested additional support, followed by group discussion, more class time, and workshops. A total of 51 of the 65 students who answered (78%) named at least one of four forms of support: worked examples, group discussion, more class time, or a workshop.

“Group discussion and worked examples to develop individual critical thinking rather than outsourcing to AI.”

Student 27

“Class time and discussions helped more than the chatbot did.”

Student 66

Student perspectives on timing

Student 57: “the chat bot was nice but would have been more effective if we'd used it in tutorials together and if it was introduced prior to ‘evaluate your source, then use the chatbot to evaluate your source,’ because we already evaluated our source at that point so it felt redundant.”

Student 72 described the same gap differently: by the time the agent was useful for the verbal assessment, the class had moved on and “we weren't yet practiced enough in using it that it was an obvious go to for that task.” The concentration of use shown earlier is the same pattern, observed from the students' side.

Would it work in other courses?

Most students were open to it: 23% said yes definitely, 31% yes for some tasks, and 31% maybe depending on how it was set up. Thirteen percent said probably not or no.

When asked where a similar agent might be useful, students most commonly identified research-based assignments, essays, and reports. Several also suggested tasks involving structured frameworks, reflection, or feedback on written work.. research-based assignments (12 students), essays and reports (9). The second most common answer, from ten students, was finding or filtering sources — a function the agent does not provide. Five imagined it giving feedback on drafts before submission. Three named framework-based work specifically, including Te Tiriti and cultural safety assignments, and one named clinical practice.

“The setup that made a difference was having the framework elements already loaded into the chatbot so I didn't have to explain them each time.”

Student 60

Anything else?

Most had nothing further to add: 31 of the 54 who answered said “no” or similar, and seven closed with thanks or an endorsement. Six gave a position on AI in education, which the earlier questions had not asked for directly. Across the whole survey, 5 students expressed concerns about the role of AI in higher education. These concerns included the potential impact on critical thinking, academic integrity, and the broader direction of educational practice.

“I don't agree with the direction this sort of method guides education towards, teach critical thinking skills rather than how to bypass the need for it.”

Student 27

“It was kind of a last resort, I didn't know how to access it at first.”

Student 15

Semester 2 usage patterns

USAGE RECORDS · SEMESTER 2 ONLY · 22 JULY TO 30 AUGUST 2026

Semester 2 is reported here using the same measures as Semester 1. The comparison between semesters is presented in the following section. The dataset comprised 339 conversations and 5,558 messages from 132 of the 442 enrolled students (30% of the cohort) with the assessment due Friday 21 August 2026. There was no Semester 2 survey, so everything in this section comes from usage records.

Use across the semester

Use increased steadily throughout the assessment period and peaked in the week beginning 17 August, which accounted for 47% of the semester's conversations. 32% of conversations occurred in the final three days before submission, while 24% took place more than two weeks before the due date..

When students used it

As in Semester 1, use extended beyond standard working hours. Fifty-seven percent of conversations began outside weekday working hours, and 50 students (38% of users) never accessed the agent during those times. The busiest single hour was midday.

How students used the agent

Step-by-step guidance was the most common mode of use, accounting for 53% of conversations. Feedback on a completed table represented 29% of conversations, while requests to improve a partial evaluation accounted for a further 8%. Guided conversations were also longer, with a median length of 14 messages.

Use outside the agent's intended purpose

Thirty-two conversations (9% of the semester) were about something other than source evaluation, mostly other parts of the report rather than work meant for a different tool. Use related to the assessment Section 3 Copilot Chat prompt, which had been common in Semester 1, did not appear in any Semester 2 conversations.

Comparison across semesters

USAGE RECORDS · BOTH SEMESTERS

The two semesters are set side by side here, on usage records only. The agent remained unchanged across the two semesters. However, the teaching context differed, including cohort size, timing within the academic year, and course coordination.

Differences between the semesters

Three differences were particularly noticeable between the semesters. Step-by-step guidance increased from 20% of Semester 1 conversations to 53% of Semester 2 conversations. The proportion of students who used this mode at least once increased from 34% in Semester 1 to 64% in Semester 2. The proportion of conversations focused on feedback for completed work fell from 61% to 29%. And students began earlier, 24% of Semester 2 conversations began more than two weeks before the due date, compared with 13% in Semester 1.

Measures that did not differ

Two measures were effectively identical. Students progressed equally far through the four elements once guided (51% covered all four in Semester 1, 54% in Semester 2) and stopped at the same point in both, between the first element and the second. After-hours use was also similar, at 51% and 57%.

The consistency of this pattern across both semesters suggests that it may relate to the design of the task, the structure of the guided interaction, or both. The usage data alone cannot determine the reason.

Questions related to Section 3 Copilot Chat prompt of the asssessment, which appeared 151 times in Semester 1, appears not once in Semester 2. Use outside the agent's intended purpose fell from 12% to 9%, and what remained was mostly reflection writing rather than work meant for another tool. The absence of these conversations in Semester 2 suggests that the distinction between the Source Evaluator and the assessment section involving the use of Copilot may have been communicated differently. The usage data show the change, but not the reason for it.

Limitations of the comparison

These differences may partly reflect the change in course lead, but the cohorts differed substantially in size, with 1,133 students enrolled in Semester 1 and 442 in Semester 2, ran at different points in the year, and may differ in ways these records cannot show. With no Semester 2 survey, nothing in this section can speak to what students made of the agent, only to what they did with it.

A narrower but useful conclusion can still be drawn. The same agent, implemented in a different teaching context and with a different cohort, was associated with substantially different patterns of use. Step-by-step guidance was available in both semesters. In Semester 1, 259 students (34% of users) engaged with step-by-step guidance at least once. In Semester 2, 84 students (64% of users) did so, and students also began using the agent earlier in the assessment period.

Conclusions

The agent achieved substantial uptake in Semester 1 and provided flexible access across both semesters

Te Whatu Aho Rau Source Evaluator achieved substantial uptake in Semester 1, with 751 students (66% of the cohort) using it at least once. Uptake was lower in Semester 2, at approximately 30% of the cohort. In both semesters, more than half of conversations occurred outside standard working hours: 51% in Semester 1 and 57% in Semester 2. This indicates that the agent extended access to assessment support beyond the times when staffed library services were available.

Students used the agent for both feedback and learning

Students engaged with the agent in different ways depending on their needs. Feedback on completed evaluations was the dominant mode of use in Semester 1, while step-by-step guidance became the most common mode in Semester 2. Together, these patterns suggest that students valued both formative feedback and scaffolded support for learning how to apply the Te Whatu Aho Rau framework.

Students generally found the agent helpful, but not sufficient on its own

Most survey respondents reported that the agent helped them learn source evaluation and navigate the assessment requirements. However, students consistently described the agent as one component of a broader support environment rather than a substitute for teaching. Requests for worked examples, discussion opportunities, workshops, and additional class time suggest that students valued the agent most when it complemented other forms of learning support.

Teaching context influenced how students used the agent

The comparison between semesters showed that the same agent was associated with different patterns of use in different teaching contexts. Students engaged earlier in Semester 2, made greater use of the guided learning mode, and were less likely to use the agent for tasks outside its intended purpose. While the usage data cannot explain why these differences occurred, the findings suggest that how an agent is introduced and integrated into teaching influences how students engage with it.

Students need support to navigate multiple AI tools

The Semester 1 conversations revealed some uncertainty about the distinction between the Source Evaluator and Copilot Chat, with a number of students attempting to use the Source Evaluator for a different assessment task. This suggests that when multiple AI tools are used within a course, students benefit from clear guidance about the purpose, scope, and limitations of each tool.

The agent expanded access to learning support

Across both semesters, many students accessed the agent outside normal working hours, including evenings and weekends. For a substantial proportion of users, the agent provided support at times when traditional services were not available. Accessibility and flexibility therefore appear to be important aspects of the agent's value.

Students largely engaged with the agent responsibly

Concerns that students would use the agent to complete assessment tasks on their behalf were not supported by the usage records. Very few conversations involved attempts to have the agent generate assessment content. Instead, students predominantly used the tool to seek guidance, feedback, clarification, and support in applying the framework. The findings suggest that Cogniti AI agents can support information literacy development when designed to scaffold student thinking rather than replace it.

Recommendations

Integrate the agent earlier into teaching activities

Introduce the agent before students begin the source evaluation component of the assessment. Earlier exposure may encourage students to engage with the guided learning functions before they reach the final stages of assignment preparation.

Position the agent as part of a wider support ecosystem

Continue presenting the agent alongside lectures, tutorials, workshops, library support, and other learning resources. Students consistently indicated that they found the agent most useful when it complemented, rather than replaced, other forms of support.

Increase visibility of the guided learning mode

Explicitly demonstrate the step-by-step guidance mode during teaching sessions and explain how it differs from feedback on completed work. This may encourage students to engage with the learning-focused features earlier in the assessment process.

Develop worked examples alongside the agent

Provide worked examples that demonstrate how the Te Whatu Aho Rau framework can be applied to source evaluation tasks. Survey respondents frequently requested examples, suggesting that they would be a valuable complement to the agent's question-based guidance.

Clarify the purposes of different AI tools

Provide clear explanations of the role, strengths, limitations, and intended uses of the Source Evaluator and Copilot Chat. Distinguishing between tools may help students select the most appropriate resource for a particular task.

Continue developing AI literacy and transparency

Support students to critically evaluate AI-generated responses and understand the limitations of AI tools. The agent should continue to be grounded in course resources and make the basis of its guidance visible to users.

Continue evaluation in future offerings

Continue monitoring usage patterns and student perceptions in future iterations of the course. Collecting comparable survey data across multiple semesters would strengthen future evaluations and help identify which aspects of implementation have the greatest influence on student engagement.
